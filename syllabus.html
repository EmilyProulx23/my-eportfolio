Course Description

ENC 3314 CAH-WRITE RHET 3(3,0) Writing and Rhetoric Foundations: PR: Grade of C (2.0) or better in ENC 1102 or equivalent. Introduction to and application of rhetorical skills and concepts that enable advanced (inter)disciplinary writing and research within and beyond the university. Fall, Spr, Sum.

ENC 3314: Writing and Rhetoric Foundations will introduce students to some of the key concepts, values, and habits of mind associated with the academic discipline known as Writing Studies (or, alternatively, "Rhetoric and Composition" or "Composition Studies"). You might be asking yourself something along the lines of, "What is there to study about writing?" While activities such as writing and reading can seem intuitive, it turns out they are more complex than they might initially seem. These activities are highly contextual and bound up with identities and values in important ways. To investigate writing and rhetoric is to examine how individuals interpret, think, communicate, argue, and make meaning as they move through the world. This course, then, will teach you to think like a writing and rhetoric scholar to become a more empowered and more critical writer, reader, and thinker yourself.

As we will see, our understandings of writing, rhetoric, language, and literacy are neither static nor universal. This semester, then, we will consider our ethical responsibility as communicators, audiences, and individuals with writing expertise to understand and critically interrogate the consequences of how particular conceptions of writing and language are constructed, imposed, and valued (or not). In learning about some of the threshold concepts of writing studies, you'll come to understand better the values, areas of expertise, questions, and boundaries of this discipline. And in learning about—and practicing—rhetoric, you'll discover ways to use communication to intervene in and shape situations that matter to you.

You might be wondering what the "Foundations" in the course title refers to. In short, this course is intended to serve two student populations:

  • Students in the Writing and Rhetoric major or minor who are early in their coursework for these programs. For these students, this course will provide some foundational concepts, methods, and practice in writing and rhetoric that will be built upon in later coursework.
  • Students from other majors taking this course as an elective. For these students, this course will serve as an introduction to some of the ideas from the discipline of writing studies, with a focus on strategies and tools that they can use to interrogate, investigate, and succeed on their own terms in the writing situations they may encounter in their future, either in the university or in other professional, civic, social, and/or personal contexts.

While this course will introduce students to some of the key concepts and questions from the field of writing studies—including looking at some academic research on writing, rhetoric, and literacy—our focus will not be on theory. Instead, we will look to this research for ideas we can take up and use ourselves, both in our writing this semester and beyond. Along the way, you'll begin to craft a working identity for yourself as a writing professional. Think of the guiding question of this course, then, as, "What can studying writing and rhetoric do for you?"

Student Learning Outcomes

After successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

  • Generate an understanding of Writing and Rhetoric disciplinary perspectives, including the research and processes used to develop those perspectives.
  • Evaluate Writing and Rhetoric scholarship through relevant disciplinary venues, research methods, and ongoing scholarly conversations.
  • Examine how different definitions of rhetoric shape the study of Writing and Rhetoric.
  • Practice applying rhetorical concepts—such as rhetorical situation, genre, transfer, reflection—strategically to support their own writing processes and knowledge construction.
  • Develop a reflective, aspirational writerly identity for effective communication across platforms, including an ePortfolio/professional website.

Major Assignment 1: The Writerly Identity Excavation

Your writerly identity has been shaped by teachers, family, languages, communities, platforms, and institutions, most of it without much choice in the matter. This assignment asks you to stop and examine that shaping deliberately, using the threshold concepts of Writing and Rhetoric as your analytical tools — then use that understanding to sketch the writer you're working to become. See the full assignment →

Major Assignment 2: Rhetorical Concept Map and Reflection

Rhetorical concepts don't just emerge and exist as static definitions. Rather, they develop through conversation across our discipline, and definitions, applications, and relevance can vary depending on the scholar or the time period. This assignment asks you to trace how one rhetorical concept develops across the discipline. By mapping this concept visually and writing a reflective analysis, you will explore how scholars define, apply, and debate the concept, and connect these insights to your own professional stance as a writer and rhetorician. Possible concepts include (but are not limited to): rhetorical situation, genre, exigence, audience, transfer, reflection, writing, rhetoric, and design.

Major Assignment 3: Professional Mini-Folio

Start—or continue developing—a professional website and online identity that can grow with you during your time at UCF and beyond, with an emphasis on showcasing your work as a writer and communicator. In particular, consider how you can translate your experiences in this class—as well as any other relevant writing or communication experiences you've had as part of other coursework, internships, jobs, etc.—for an audience of your choosing. This audience could include potential employers, internships, and graduate programs, depending on your individual goals.

State Writing Requirement

You must earn at least a C− or higher to fulfill the university and state requirements. All course assignments are evaluated and will be included in the final course grade. To fulfill this requirement, you must complete a minimum of 4 written assignments worth at least 60% of the final grade; engage in substantial, developed writing of at least one multi-page writing assignment; and revise at least one assignment in response to substantive teacher feedback. The qualifying assignments are: at least 7 Online Activities, Major Project 1, Major Project 2, and Major Project 3. Fulfilling the requirement does not by itself guarantee a passing grade.

Expectations: What Does Each Grade Look Like?

  • A: Includes all requirements listed in the description; engages thoughtfully with course content and includes original ideas, interpretations, and/or arguments. Sources follow the conventions for effective textual support outlined in the course materials.
  • B: Includes all requirements and engages with course content but may not extend beyond the minimum requirement. Fulfills the goals of the learning activity with room for improvement.

  • D: May be missing more than one requirement, but still partially fulfills the goals of the learning activity.
  • F: Does not fulfill the goals of the learning activity — from a lack of following directions, missing material, or a lack of evidence that you completed your own work.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) Use Policy

Definition of Generative AI

Generative AI tools use predictive technology to produce new text, charts, images, audio, video, and other media. This includes not only ChatGPT and similar Large Language Models (LLMs), but also writing assistants, image generators, transcription/translation tools, code assistants, and plug-ins built on this or similar AI technologies.

Policy

Writing is integral to thinking. It is also hard. Generative AI applications like ChatGPT or Gemini are useful tools for helping us improve our writing and stimulate our thinking. However, they should never serve as a substitute for either. And, in this course, they cannot.

Think of the help you get from generative AI as a much less sophisticated version of the assistance you can receive (for free!) from a UCF Writing Center tutor. That person might legitimately ask you a question to jump-start your imagination, steer you away from the passive voice, or identify a poorly organized paragraph, but should never do the writing for you. A major difference here, of course, is that an AI app is not a person. It's a machine that is adept at recognizing patterns and reflecting those patterns back at us. It cannot think for itself. And it cannot think for you.

With that analogy in mind, you will need to adhere to the following guidelines in our class.

✓ Appropriate use of AI

  • Spell check, grammar check, and synonym identification tools (e.g., Grammarly, MS Word).
  • App recommendations for rephrasing sentences or reorganizing paragraphs you have drafted yourself.
  • App recommendations for tweaking outlines you have drafted yourself.
  • AI tools for research support (e.g., locating sources, summarizing background material) as long as you verify and read the original sources yourself before citing them.
  • Assistive and accessibility tools — including text-to-speech, speech-to-text, translation, and reading-support AI — to support how you read, draft, or revise. These are available to any student who finds them useful; you do not need a formal, documented accommodation through Student Accessibility Services to use them in this course. (If you do have a documented accommodation, this policy does not limit or replace it — SAS accommodations always apply.)
  • If a specific assignment permits AI-generated images, audio, or video (e.g., as illustration or multimedia support rather than as the writing itself), you may use them within the scope defined in that assignment's instructions.

✕ Inappropriate use of AI

  • Using entire sentences or paragraphs suggested by an app without quotation marks and a citation, just as you would for any other source. (Example citation: OpenAI, ChatGPT. Response to prompt: "Explain what is meant by the term 'Triple Bottom Line'" (February 15, 2023, https://chat.openai.com/).)
  • Having an app write a draft (either rough or final) of an assignment for you.
  • Using AI-generated images, audio, or video as a substitute for your own writing or original work, unless a specific assignment explicitly permits it.
  • Inputting any work not your own into an AI tool — including your classmates' work, the authors we read, and even the content I've created for the course.
  • Depending on the scope of the usage and the parameters of the assignment, submitting work produced by AI may constitute academic dishonesty. This may result in revising and resubmitting the work or taking a zero for the assignment, evaluated case by case.

Disclosure

Different organizations are still deciding how they want to go about the disclosure of the use of Generative AI. For our purposes, we'll follow the American Psychological Association, as APA is one of the leading professional organizations, and many other professional organizations follow its guidelines. Guiding principles:

  • Be transparent, providing the necessary information for readers to understand how generative AI was used during the research and drafting process. Disclose the use in the most relevant place in the manuscript.
  • Maintain ethical approaches to using generative AI, including not entering confidential material into open generative AI tools.
  • Take responsibility for the information provided by generative AI tools, including verifying information by reading the original sources used in AI output.
  • Provide attribution to the generative AI tool where it was used to generate ideas, content, analysis, code, or research elements — if you would credit a human contributor for a similar activity, credit the tool.
  • Disclosure format depends on the genre: sometimes a reflection, sometimes a citation or footnote. Pay careful attention to individual assignment requirements.

Technology Disclosure Statement (required with every assignment)

Because this course treats writing as a process shaped by the tools we use to think, research, draft, and revise, every major assignment must be submitted with a brief Technology Disclosure Statement (3–5 sentences, or a short list). In it, you should:

  • Name the technologies you used at each stage of the assignment (drafting, research, revision, editing, formatting, etc.) — this includes generative AI, but also things like citation managers, grammar/style checkers, translation tools, or other software that shaped your process.
  • Briefly describe how you used each tool (e.g., "used [tool] to generate an outline I then rewrote," "used [tool] to check citation formatting," "used [tool] to find sources on X, which I read and verified myself").
  • Note anything you revised or rejected based on a tool's suggestion — this is often the most interesting part of the reflection, not just what you used.

This statement is a reflection and transparency exercise, not a confession. Using technology well and disclosing it honestly will not lower your grade. What affects your grade is whether the final work meets the assignment's learning goals and reflects your own thinking.

Exception: Use of assistive or accessibility technology (text-to-speech, speech-to-text, translation for personal comprehension, reading support, etc.) does not need to be named in the Technology Disclosure Statement. These tools support how you access and process material rather than what you're claiming as your own thinking, and requiring students to name them would work against the goal of making them freely available to anyone who benefits from them, accommodation or not.

If you are ever unsure whether the way you are using technology or online tools constitutes academic dishonesty, or whether something belongs in your disclosure statement, please don't hesitate to ask.
