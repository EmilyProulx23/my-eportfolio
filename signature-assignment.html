Signature Assignment · ENC 3314, Unit 1

The Writerly Identity Excavation

A multimodal project that asks students to examine how they became the writers they are — and sketch the writers they're working to become.

Instructor Context

This is a first-time prep of ENC 3314: Writing and Rhetoric Foundations, delivered fully asynchronously online, as part of the rollout of our new curriculum for the Writing and Rhetoric BA — a curriculum adding more digital competencies and a richer understanding of how rhetoric applies interdisciplinarily. The population is mixed: Writing and Rhetoric majors and minors alongside students completing the professional writing certificate, which means uneven prior exposure to Writing Studies as a discipline. The assignment is written to assume no prior theoretical fluency and to treat the five threshold concepts as entry points rather than review.

Pedagogically, this assignment is the culmination of Unit 1, before students have engaged deeply with disciplinary scholarship and research methods (Unit 2) or with competing definitions of rhetoric (Unit 3). Because it's early, the emphasis is on building comfort with the vocabulary and stance of the field — naming concepts and applying them to something the student already knows intimately — rather than evaluating scholarship they haven't encountered yet. It also builds community in the course, with multiple chances to workshop and share work. The assignment works toward these course outcomes:

  • Generate an understanding of Writing and Rhetoric disciplinary perspectives, including the research and processes used to develop those perspectives.
  • Practice applying rhetorical concepts — such as rhetorical situation, genre, transfer, reflection — strategically to support their own writing processes and knowledge construction.
  • Develop a reflective, aspirational writerly identity for effective communication across platforms, including an ePortfolio/professional website.

Unit readings supporting the assignment include:

  • Devitt, A. J. (2004). Chapter 2, "An analysis of genres in social settings." In Writing Genres (pp. 33–65). Southern Illinois University Press.
  • Meyer, J. H. F., & Land, R. (2005). "Threshold concepts and troublesome knowledge: Epistemological considerations and a conceptual framework for teaching and learning." Higher Education, 49(3), 373–388.
  • Naming What We Know, "Concept 1: Writing is a Social and Rhetorical Activity."
  • Scott, J. B., & Wardle, E. (2015). "Using threshold concepts to inform writing and rhetoric undergraduate majors: The UCF experiment." In L. Adler-Kassner & E. Wardle (Eds.), Naming what we know: Threshold concepts of writing studies (pp. 122–140).
  • Yancey, K. B. (2018). "Mapping the turn to disciplinarity: A historical analysis of composition's trajectory and its current movement." In R. Malenczyk et al. (Eds.), Composition, Rhetoric, and Disciplinarity (pp. 15–35).

Purpose

Your writerly identity has been shaped by teachers, family, languages, communities, platforms, and institutions, most of it without much choice in the matter. This assignment asks you to stop and examine that shaping deliberately, using the threshold concepts of Writing and Rhetoric as your analytical tools.

Threshold concepts are ideas that, once understood, change how you see an entire field. Wardle and Adler-Kassner's Naming What We Know identifies five threshold concepts of writing that anchor this course:

  • Writing is a social and rhetorical activity.
  • Writing speaks to situations through recognizable forms.
  • Writing enacts and creates identities and ideologies.
  • All writers have more to learn.
  • Writing is (also always) a cognitive activity.

This assignment asks you to use these concepts to interrogate your writerly history: the people, places, languages, and experiences that shaped who you are as a writer. What did they teach you that writing was? Whose writing did they hold up as the standard, and whose did they ignore? What did they reward, and what did they punish? What did you internalize that you have never examined? What resources did they give you, and what did they withhold?

While excavation often only looks backward, this course asks you to think forward, too. Once you've unearthed how you became this kind of writer, use that understanding to sketch the writer you're working to become. The goal is a more critical, specific, theoretically grounded account of your writerly identity — past, present, and aspirational — that will orient you for the rest of the course and lay groundwork for the ePortfolio you'll build later this semester.

Task

You will produce two artifacts for this assignment:

1. The Excavation

A multimodal piece that examines your writerly history and gestures toward your aspirational writerly identity. The form is yours to choose, and should be the form that best fits what you have to say. Possibilities include (but are not limited to): a zine, an annotated photo essay, a designed document, an audio piece with visual accompaniment, a short video essay, an illustrated map, a digital collage, or an annotated archive of past writing. If you have another form in mind, propose it.

You will propose your excavation genre during Week 3. You must have an approved proposal before submitting this final assignment.

Accessibility Requirements

Because this is an async online course and the Excavation's form is open, accessibility is your responsibility as the designer, not an afterthought. Whatever form you choose:

  • Images need alt text or an equivalent written description.
  • Audio and video need captions or a transcript.
  • Color should not be the only way information is conveyed (for colorblind and low-vision readers).
  • Submit in a file format your classmates and instructor can actually open (check before you finalize a niche tool or plugin).

If you're unsure how to make your chosen form accessible, make use of office hours and the weekly modules for help.

2. The Designer's Reflection

A companion piece (750–1000 words) in which you:

  • Explain the rhetorical choices you made in the Excavation — audience, purpose, genre — and why the form you chose was the right vehicle for your argument.
  • Engage explicitly with course material, naming the threshold concepts or readings that shaped your thinking.
  • Provide a technological disclosure: a candid account of every tool you used to make the Excavation (including generative AI), including what you used it for, and how it shaped what you made.
  • Reflect on transfer: name at least one thing from this project (a concept, a practice, a question) that you expect to carry into future writing situations, including the ePortfolio you'll build later in the course.

This reflection should be in MLA or APA format with specific references to Naming What We Know and other course readings.

Criteria

Strong work on this assignment will:

  • Use at least three threshold concepts accurately and specifically, with evidence drawn from your own history rather than a generic summary.
  • Choose a form for the Excavation that is a genuine rhetorical choice, and defend that choice in the Designer's Reflection.
  • Move beyond narration to analysis: both pieces should ask "why" and "so what," not just "what happened."
  • Look forward as well as back, sketching an aspirational writerly identity alongside the examination of the past.
  • Include a complete, honest technological disclosure.
  • Meet accessibility requirements.
  • Follow the AI use guidelines below, disclosed honestly in the Designer's Reflection.

Timeline & Scaffolding

This project is scaffolded across several weeks, so you have low-stakes room to experiment before anything is graded.

  1. Week 2 — Play week

    Low-stakes, ungraded experimentation with two or three multimodal tools or genres (e.g., a quick zine page, a 30-second audio clip, a mock-up in a design tool).

  2. Week 3 — Proposal

    Continue exploring tools. Submit a short topic-and-medium proposal (which experiences you're excavating, which threshold concepts you expect to use, and what form you're leaning toward) for instructor feedback.

  3. Week 4 — Draft development

    Work-in-progress check-in on the Excavation.

  4. Week 5 — Final due

    Excavation and Designer's Reflection due.

Generative AI Use

Generative AI tools are part of the landscape you write in, and this course does not ask you to pretend otherwise. Rather than banning these tools, this assignment asks you to think about how to use them without letting them replace the thinking and learning you're here to do. The core principle: AI cannot do your thinking for you. It is a tool, not a replacement — a distinction that connects directly to threshold concept 5, writing is (also always) a cognitive activity. If a tool does your thinking, you skip the cognitive work this assignment is designed to build.

✓ Appropriate use

  • Using AI to help you brainstorm which experiences from your history feel most analytically rich.
  • Asking AI to explain what a course concept means before you apply it to your own experience.
  • Getting feedback on whether a sentence is clear.
  • Using AI to look up what a particular tool or genre convention is called.
  • Asking AI to suggest multimodal forms you hadn't considered, then deciding for yourself whether they fit your argument.
  • If your Excavation involves code (e.g., a website or interactive piece), you may use AI to help you write or debug that code.

✕ Not appropriate

  • Asking AI to generate the analysis of your writerly history.
  • Using AI to produce the written content of your Designer's Reflection.
  • Having AI decide what your claim is or how your project should be structured.
  • Using AI as a substitute for engaging with the course readings.
  • Submitting any AI-generated text as your own analytical writing.

How to tell the difference

Ask yourself: did I do the thinking, and did the tool help me express or refine it? Or did the tool do the thinking, and am I just presenting its output? Remember, the goal here is for you to learn how to reflect and analyze your own experiences, so you cannot outsource the decision-making or analysis to an AI tool. Disclose your AI use honestly in the technological disclosure.
